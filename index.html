We Need Graphic Design Histories
That Look Beyond the Profession

Words by Aggie Toppins. Published on June 10th, 2021

Graphic design history is, generally speaking, a history of Professional practice. In text books, at conferences, and for college courses, design historians and educators explain the development of the industry through the example of pivotal figures, canonical works, and dominant styles. And yet, visual communication is not only a professional domain. It’s an active cultural practice shared by all. What would it look like to expand the scope of history beyond professional boundaries, and what might we gain from doing this?

Pivotal figures, Canonical works, Dominant styles & An active cultural practice shared by all.

1922: WA Dwiggins coined the term “graphic designer” after World War II. Widely used
1960: Graphic design become a subject for academic study across Europe and the US
1980: personal computer and desktop publishing >> professionalize graphic design history made its debut.
1985: Philip B. Meggs, AIGA Journal of Graphic Design: Framed graphic design through the rhetoric of problem-solving and argued for design history as a means of professional legitimation.

Okay... but how do we actually do that?

What stood out to me the most was the author's argument that we should not confine ourselves to so-called professional design practices, but expand our perspective and look at design on a broader historical scale, seeing designing as an activity that can exist beyond the professional field.

I think my own learning path has been similar.

As someone without a design background, I started out always trying to make cool posters with cool styles. Gradually, I realized that good design is not only about how it looks, but also about whether it can convey a concept clearly or solve a problem. I guess if I had realized this earlier, I wouldn't have felt so insecure about myself as a designer. At the same time, though, creating a distinctive visual style is still something I enjoy pursuing.

Even though I felt more confident after reading this article, it also made me realize something else: maybe this is part of why graphic designers can sometimes be underestimated where I come from? If we understand design broadly as a problem-solving activity, then in some sense, ANYONE CAN DESIGN?

For example, my uncle once simply used a screenshot of a conversation about not throwing trash everywhere as a poster. I actually considered it a great design because it conveyed the idea clearly, and it looked so different from other posters that it immediately caught people's attention.

The question I still have is: if we don’t define professional design simply by its style or by recognition from professional institutions, and if design is something anyone can participate in, what distinguishes the value of a professional designer?
